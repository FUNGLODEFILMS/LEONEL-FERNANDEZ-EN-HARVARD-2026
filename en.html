About the event

Former president Leonel Fernández spent an hour at Harvard discussing democracy in Latin America, the Dominican diaspora and the shifting geopolitical order. This report summarizes his remarks in the order they came up.

03 / EVENT REPORT

Introduction and personal background

A Harvard College student on the forum committee introduced Fernández. She noted that at 42 he became the youngest president in Dominican history and that he served three terms. He has also led the World Federation of United Nations Associations, the EU-LAC Foundation and the Fuerza del Pueblo party, and founded FUNGLODE (Global Foundation for Democracy and Development).

Kayyem opened by asking how he became president without planning to. Fernández joked that it came only after three earlier "failures":

Baseball player. He wanted to follow Juan Marichal, whom he watched pitch at Shea Stadium, but lacked the talent.

Journalist. He worked unpaid; after a colleague disappeared, his mother made him stop.

UN official. An Egyptian diplomat friend promised him a job after law school, but died before he graduated.

On his years in New York, from age 8 to 16, he said learning English and reading in it gave him a perspective that complemented his Spanish-language one. He lived on the Upper West Side and attended public schools in Manhattan until early high school. Returning to the Dominican Republic, he saw the gap between abundance and scarcity, which he said shaped his drive for change at home.

The moderator said the evening would look beyond Fernández and the Dominican Republic to the region and a changing world order. The official introduction set two themes: the state of democracy in Latin America and how the situation in the Middle East is affecting the region.

Fernández added that shuttling between New York and Santo Domingo drew him into Dominican political debate early. That dual experience, he explained, gave him a different reading of politics, economics and life, and a close view of what modernization looks like.

04 / EVENT REPORT

Democratic stability in the Dominican Republic

Fernández argued the country has had nearly 50 years of stable democracy because no one now imagines reaching power except through elections. He described recent history in stages:

Failed transition (1961–1965). After Trujillo's death, Juan Bosch won by a wide margin but governed only seven months. The 1965 uprising to restore him ended with a U.S. intervention that treated it as a communist revolt; Fernández called sending the troops a mistake.

Semi-repressive cycle (1966–1996). Joaquín Balaguer, the last president of the Trujillo era, won disputed elections in 1966 and dominated a 30-year cycle.

Turning point (1978). The opposition's victory that year, in his view, began the real democratic transition for the country and the wider region.

Consolidation (1996 onward). Since he first took office, he said, no one has sought to reverse the democratic process.

He credited this stability to the quality of leadership: Balaguer as a national legend, Bosch as the intellectual who shaped his generation, and José Francisco Peña Gómez as a lasting reference though never president. He added that democracy is more than voting: governments must deliver basics such as food, electricity, water, housing and public safety.

Kayyem pressed on whether this model could be replicated in countries that swing between extremes. Fernández placed the 1965 intervention in a Cold War frame: the Johnson administration read as pro-Cuban a revolution he considers democratic.

He acknowledged that Dominican elections, held every four years, have at times been disputed and even marred by fraud. Still, he argued there is a broad consensus that economic growth and sustainable development require a stable democratic system. Neither military coups nor guerrilla warfare, he said, are now seen as legitimate routes to power.

The moderator noted that the country, the largest economy in its area, shows the model can work.

Migration: the diaspora and Haiti

The diaspora as a development partner

Fernández proposed a Ministry of Dominicans Abroad to tie the diaspora to a 30-year national project. He cited these figures:

The goal is for the diaspora to contribute beyond family remittances. He named three channels:

Housing. The state would provide land to lower costs and guarantee transparency and legal security for investors.

Public safety. Dominican officers in U.S. forces could help with crime prevention. He recalled sending officers to train in criminal investigation at John Jay College during his government.

Knowledge economy. Diaspora doctors, engineers, architects, judges and teachers would help move the country from a labor-intensive model to one with more added value and technology.

Here he greeted the Dominicans in the audience and Dominican Harvard professor Juan Ariel Jiménez.

Kayyem asked whether this meant the diaspora funding large projects rather than only supporting relatives. Fernández clarified that remittances will continue and today underpin the country's economic stability; the new element would be organized investment and human capital.

On public safety, he said Dominican police can usually identify those responsible for a crime within about 48 hours, but prevention is now the challenge and one of the country's most serious problems. That is where he sees value in the experience of Dominican-origin officers in the U.S.

He also listed supermarket owners, health professionals and other diaspora groups as resources to bring in. In his view, this partnership would be the deepest positive change of the next 30-year historical cycle.

Haiti

Fernández noted that Dominican independence was won from Haiti, not from a European colonial power, which explains a complicated relationship. He said cooperation worked well during his time in office with a democratic Haitian president who completed two terms.

Since the assassination of Jovenel Moïse, he described a political vacuum in which criminal gangs hold power, with kidnappings, torture and disappearances. That, he said, forces the country to secure the border and protect its frontier provinces. He defended the sovereign right to deport irregular migrants.

He rejected claims that the country creates stateless people. Dominican nationality passes by descent (jus sanguinis), as in most countries, not by birthplace as in the U.S. A child born in the country to foreign parents can be registered at the parents' consulate, so the child is not left without a nationality.

From context, the Haitian president he described working well with was René Préval, the only one to complete two democratic terms. Fernández said deportations have always taken place but have increased because of Haiti's crisis.

On nationality, he said Americans often assume a law that differs from U.S. birthright rules must be wrong, when most countries follow descent. Once this was clarified, he argued, the statelessness accusation against the country lost force.

06 / EVENT REPORT

Corruption and climate change

Corruption

Fernández framed anti-corruption work as a matter of values and of legal tools. His government passed anti-corruption laws, stepped up enforcement and promoted ethics in public service. He acknowledged that treating public office as a path to personal gain still persists. It has not been eliminated, he said, but there is now an expectation that wrongdoing carries consequences.

Kayyem framed corruption as a threat to democracy well beyond the region. Fernández stressed that a leader must see himself as representing the national interest, and that this ethical awareness should reach every public post. He presented the fight against corruption as unfinished work for the years ahead.

Climate change

He described climate change as a threat across the Caribbean: more frequent hurricanes, rain outside the usual season, and recent earthquakes, including two the week before the forum. He pointed to risks for food production, industry and tourism. He warned of a likely return of El Niño and called for adaptation and disaster-recovery policies.

He noted the recent earthquakes were not limited to the Dominican Republic; Venezuela, Colombia and Peru also had them. He called El Niño the sharpest way extreme weather hits the region. Awareness is greater than before, he said, and each sector, especially agriculture and tourism, needs its own adaptation policies.

07 / EVENT REPORT

Latin America in the new geopolitical order

Relations with the United States

Fernández characterized the relationship as a mix of admiration and resentment: admiration for U.S. progress, resentment over past interventions. The Clinton, Bush and Obama administrations pushed free trade, but the hemisphere-wide agreement never materialized. Only Mexico, Central America and the Caribbean, the Dominican Republic among them, signed deals.

His core argument: in the 1990s and 2000s U.S. companies left South America for China, and Chinese investment then flowed into South America. ECLAC regards 2003–2013 as the region's golden decade, with average annual growth of 5.5%, driven by a China growing 12% a year and buying oil, gas and copper. When Chinese growth slowed to 4% after 2014, the region fell to 0.5% until the pandemic.

The U.S., he said, now wants to regain ground through a "Trump corollary," modeled on Theodore Roosevelt's, to keep outside powers from gaining influence. He sees this as more feasible in the nearby, more dependent Caribbean than in South America.

Kayyem framed the question this way: U.S. relations with Latin America have changed over the past two years, like it or not, raising doubts about how reliable a partner the U.S. now is. Fernández explained why South America differs: it depends less on the U.S. economy, is farther away, and its exports lean toward Asia. He therefore expects greater U.S. involvement there to be slower and harder than in the Caribbean.

Venezuela

He recalled that Venezuela was a democratic model after 1958. In his view, IMF adjustment programs without social safeguards under Carlos Andrés Pérez triggered the Caracazo riots and opened the way for Hugo Chávez. With oil rising from US$15 to US$147 a barrel between 2000 and 2008, Chávez funded social programs and won every election comfortably.

Nicolás Maduro, lacking those resources, turned to repression. Fernández observed the 2024 election and did not recognize it, judging it fraudulent. Even so, he believes the U.S. intervention was driven by geopolitics, meaning Venezuela's ties to Iran, China and Russia, and by oil, rather than by electoral fraud.

Kayyem asked whether this shift worries him about where the U.S. is heading over the next two years. Fernández answered with history. He recalled that the Dominican Republic and Venezuela have long been close, since Venezuela backed Dominicans against the Trujillo dictatorship.

On Chávez, he said the coup attempt failed militarily but made him a political hero, seen as the alternative to corrupt and ineffective governments. Chávez won in 1998, took office in 1999 and overlapped with Fernández's own time in office; the two knew each other.

He added that Maduro's 2014 and 2018 elections had already been questioned. He also noted that Trump makes no secret of the oil motive and speaks openly of placing Venezuelan crude under U.S. control.

The war with Iran

He said the war has made oil markets volatile, with prices above US$100 at times. The resulting inflation and social unrest tend to punish incumbents at the polls, in the U.S. as well. Drawing a parallel with the 1979–1981 hostage crisis, he suggested Iran is betting on Trump losing a chamber of Congress in the midterms. Those elections, in his view, will shape U.S. policy toward Latin America and the world.

Kayyem started from her own assessment: Iran will likely come out stronger, and passage through the Strait of Hormuz will carry conditions. Fernández developed the historical parallel: Iran held the embassy hostages throughout Carter's term and freed them right after Reagan's inauguration.

He explained why the midterms matter so much: if Democrats win even one chamber, Trump could no longer run foreign policy unilaterally and would need congressional support.

Carney's "middle powers"

He faulted Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney's proposal for leaving out small countries, which number 80 to 90 worldwide. He added that the middle powers already group together in BRICS, which by his account holds roughly 60% of oil and nearly half of global GDP.

Kayyem asked whether Latin America could act as a regional counterweight between China and the U.S. alongside middle powers such as Brazil or Canada. Fernández replied that this middle ground already exists in BRICS, so Carney's idea does not create a new space for small countries.

Drug trafficking and drone strikes

On U.S. lethal strikes against alleged traffickers, which the moderator said had killed more than 200 people, Fernández offered an alternative. First, deliver on the Colombian peace deal's promise to replace illicit crops; that did not happen, and coca production has grown. Second, share intelligence among Colombia, Peru, Venezuela, the Caribbean and the U.S. to prevent trafficking and pursue major kingpins.

The moderator described the strikes as extrajudicial killings without due process and noted that victims' families deny they were traffickers. Fernández did not call the tactic a success, stressing instead that regional coordination must combine prevention with lawful prosecution.

UN reform

Asked how the world might look in five years, he said global trends increasingly drive local political cycles. His answer is a return to multilateralism, but a reformed one:

The UN is the only body able to bring together 193 states.

The IMF need not always be led by a European, nor the World Bank by an American.

The Security Council veto reflects 1945; countries such as Brazil or South Africa should have a place.

As an example, he said that if the Iran war keeps inflation rising, incumbents will pay for it at the next election. This dependence on global forces has always existed but has sped up, and no country can handle it alone. That is why he rejects the view that the UN is useless: the problem is its design, not its existence.

Kayyem closed this part by praising how he handles disagreement as an example for students.

08 / EVENT REPORT

Audience questions

Eight attendees, mostly Harvard students, asked questions. Fernández's answers are summarized below.

Who will be the candidate in 2028, you or your son, Senator Omar Fernández? (A Kennedy School MPA student who had heard the senator at Harvard's Latin American Conference.)

After his third term ended in 2012 he planned to move on, but events led him to feel democracy and the Constitution were at risk.

He mobilized supporters, split from the PLD, a party he helped found, and created Fuerza del Pueblo.

He ran an improvised 2020 campaign to secure the party's legal status (5%), won 30% in 2024, and says he now leads polls at 42%, though the electoral process has not formally begun.

His goal is to plant a new 30-year cycle and then step aside for the next generation, which includes Omar.

How will you convince Dominicans you offer something new after 12 years in power?

It comes down to vision: he promised to make Santo Domingo a "little New York," believes he delivered, and now wants a bigger one.

He proposes adding value to exported goods and services to generate more wealth, spread it, and grow the middle class.

He points to the Santo Domingo Cyber Park (2000) and ITLA, which has trained more than 60,000 young people in software, biotech, nanotech, mechatronics and multimedia; semiconductors would be next.

The country already trades about US$15 billion a year with Florida, mainly textiles, footwear, plastics and medical devices; the aim is to join Florida's tech corridor.

With prosperity for most people, he argued, no one would put democracy at risk again, and the country could become a knowledge hub and regional model.

What can Latin America contribute to stability in Africa? (A Sudanese survivor of the Darfur genocide and UNHCR goodwill ambassador raised reports of former Colombian soldiers fighting in El Fasher, where tens of thousands died within days.)

He joked that his means are modest these days, since he is "unemployed."

The region has few direct ties to Africa, apart from Brazil's links with Angola and other Portuguese-speaking countries.

Under his government the country opened its first embassies in Morocco, Algeria, Egypt, South Africa and Kenya, and he represented Latin America at an African Union summit.

He regretted that Latin American integration is unraveling over ideological differences, but expects it to recover; he favors closer ties with Africa and peace efforts through the UN.

What three priorities should the region pursue as the U.S. and China refocus on it? (A Colombian student, who referred to a revived "Monroe" or "Donroe" doctrine.)

Democracy as the only path, with respect between rivals: a political opponent is not a personal enemy, and language should reflect that.

A shift from commodity dependence to a higher-value, technology-driven economy.

Unity so that Latin America speaks with one voice internationally.

Which sectors should investors target given the risks to tourism? Where should Puerto Rico start on corruption?

Diversify within tourism: beyond sun and beach, add eco, adventure, mountain, business, academic and heritage tourism, since Santo Domingo holds the New World's first European settlements, university and church.

Diversify the wider economy too: free-trade zones now focused on apparel and footwear could add pharmaceuticals and tech firms.

On corruption, the first step is ensuring there is no impunity and that wrongdoing against the public interest has consequences.

How can students use a Harvard education to serve the country? (A first-year student born in Moca in 2008, one of two Dominican-born students in his class.)

His party has identified the 20 most pressing economic, social and political issues and set up working groups to study best practices and design 30-year policies.

Topics include electricity, energy, water, renewables, education quality, housing and security.

He invited students to propose ideas, suggested Professor Juan Ariel Jiménez involve his students, and told the questioner he was already "on board."

Even if reformed, will the UN be effective if powers like the U.S. ignore its decisions?

Eighty-one years after its founding, the UN reflects a world that no longer exists; everyone knows it, but the five permanent members will not share the veto.

Kofi Annan, Ban Ki-moon and António Guterres all proposed reforms without success, and he expects the debate to last at least another decade.

No country can run the world alone: even Trump is asking NATO for help in the Strait of Hormuz.

Sustainable development, climate change, pandemics and wars can only be tackled multilaterally, and universities like Harvard have the standing to shape reform.

He likened the UN to Churchill's well-known remark about democracy: flawed, but worse to be without.

How can countries like the Dominican Republic keep strategic autonomy without swapping one dependence for another?

The U.S. is clearly the main partner: home to 2.5 million Dominicans and destination for 85% of exports.

Switching recognition from Taiwan to China was justified by China's weight at the UN and its influence over African votes, useful if the country seeks multilateral posts.

He compared today's U.S. stance toward China with Monroe's toward Europe in the 19th century, and expects the tension to fade over time.

Since no country in the region would serve as a platform to attack the U.S., he believes it can deal with everyone without provoking hostility.

Kayyem closed by thanking the guest for his candor and the audience for its questions.

Democratic stability in the Dominican Republic (6:36)

Kayyem: When your political career begins, not only are you a historic figure in the Dominican Republic, but one of the things that struck me, and I think it would be helpful for the audience, is that the Dominican Republic had a coup in 1963 but has remained a relatively stable democracy in the decades since. That's not a model that is easily replicated. So tell us a little bit about why you think the Dominican Republic has been able to avoid the cycles of military rule, extreme right and extreme left that we see not just in a lot of Latin America but throughout much of the world.

Fernández: I think we have gone through stages. After Trujillo's death, there was a period when the Dominican Republic was struggling for democracy. Then Juan Bosch, who was the main leader in exile against the Trujillo regime, was elected in a landslide but was in power for only seven months. So we can say that the original transition to democracy in the Dominican Republic was a failure: only seven months in office for a president elected by the majority of the people.

Then there was a popular revolt in 1965 to bring back Juan Bosch and the 1963 Constitution. Within the Cold War framework, the Johnson administration thought that the democratic revolution taking place in the Dominican Republic was a communist, pro-Cuban revolution, which it was not. I think it was a mistake to send troops to the Dominican Republic to prevent Bosch from coming back to power. So the first transition to democracy failed, and then Joaquín Balaguer, who had been the last president under Trujillo's regime, was elected in 1966 in contested elections.

So we have a historical cycle that goes from 1966 to 1996, 30 years, of a semi-repressive government. Then in 1978 we held elections and the opposition won, and I think that not only for the Dominican Republic but for all of Latin America, 1978 was a key point in a real transition to democracy in the whole region. We're almost 50 years from those events. During those 40-plus years, the Dominican Republic has been a stable democratic country, and the reason is that we have found that you don't access power through a military coup or through guerrilla warfare; the only legal and legitimate way to access power is through elections.

We hold free and fair elections every four years. Sometimes these elections are contested; sometimes there is fraud. But in general terms, I think we have understood that in order to progress, to have the economy grow and to have sustainable development, it is important to have a stable democratic political system, and we have that in the Dominican Republic.

Kayyem: It's the largest economy in the area, and it's a model that other countries can look to and say, "We can make it work." Is there something you can replicate in other countries? Because we certainly go back and forth between extremes a lot in many of these countries.

Fernández: I think in our case it has to do with the quality of leadership. During those 30 years, from 1966 to 1996, we had Balaguer, who is a national legend. You can be with him or against him, but he is a national legend. You had Juan Bosch, who was a towering intellectual figure and influenced my generation. And you had a third leader, José Francisco Peña Gómez, who never became president but was a very influential leader and is a reference in the Dominican Republic. I think their responsible, ethical leadership enabled the Dominican Republic to have a stable political system.

Then in 1996, 30 years ago, we came into office, and during the 30 years since, I think Dominican democracy has consolidated. No one is thinking of reversing the process. Everyone knows that in order to get into office, you have to go through the electoral process.

Of course, democracy is not only elections. Once you're elected, you have to govern democratically, but you also have to deliver, and deliver on basic needs: food security, electricity, water supply, housing, citizen security. These basic issues need to be addressed effectively so that people feel comfortable within a democratic system.

Because we understand that China is a rising power with a lot of influence in the UN system. African countries tend to follow China because of all the investment China has in Africa. So if we want to hold a position at the UN, which I think is important, we wouldn't have the Chinese vote otherwise. So having formal, direct diplomatic relations with China is useful for the Dominican Republic, and I think it is useful for China too.

But the atmosphere prevailing at this moment in history is that the U.S. sees China the way it saw the European countries in the 19th century, during President Monroe's term: a policy of non-interference within the hemisphere. I think that will eventually fade away. It's a question of the moment. There is no way that any country in the Caribbean or Latin America would serve as a platform for an attack on U.S. soil. It will not happen. So I think that, over time, we can overcome that and have relationships with anyone around the world without generating any animosity from the U.S.

Kayyem: Thank you, President Fernández. We all want to thank you for your time, your honesty and your intellect. It was wonderful. And thanks to the IOP and the Forum team for a lovely evening. You are all dismissed. And yes, very good questions, class. Thank you so much.

Fernández: Thank you so much.
